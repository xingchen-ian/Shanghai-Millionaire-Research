The Color Groups: Encoding Shanghai's Extraterritorial Hierarchy

V&A Waddington World of Monopoly SM board data Back Road history

Shanghai Millionaire inherits the eight-color-group structure from Waddington's 1936 London edition, whose color progression is widely recognized as encoding British social class — from working-class Whitechapel to aristocratic Mayfair. But a direct transposition of London's class logic onto Shanghai is analytically problematic: the two cities organize space along fundamentally different axes. Our research points toward a more compelling interpretation: the color-group assignments in Shanghai Millionaire encode the real spatial-nationality hierarchy that existed in 1930s Shanghai under the extraterritoriality regime. The board did not import British class structure — it materialized the power architecture of the treaty-port city itself.

The Bund vs. the Back Roads: A Spatial Binary Recognized at the Time

In 1930s Shanghai, financial geography operated on a clearly recognized dual structure. The Bund waterfront housed foreign banks — HSBC, Chartered Bank, Citibank — occupying the city's most prestigious real estate. Behind Nanjing Road, in an area known as the houmalu (后马路, "Back Roads"), Chinese-owned financial institutions concentrated: over ninety qianzhuang (traditional native banks), the Shanghai Commercial and Savings Bank (Republican China's largest private bank), and the headquarters of the Native Bankers' Association. Contemporary Chinese media explicitly labeled Ningpo Road "China's Wall Street" (《中国建筑》, 1934). This was not a neutral geographic fact — it was a spatial encoding of power under extraterritoriality.

The Hierarchy Was Real — and Documented

The spatial-nationality hierarchy encoded in the board was not an abstract colonial gaze — it was an institutional reality, documented across multiple independent sources:

Extraterritoriality
The legal architecture itself: foreigners in the concessions were not subject to Chinese law. The SMC Board excluded Chinese members until 1928.

French Concession Zoning (1934)
Official planning document divided the concession into four zones — Zone I for "native population" (mixed-use, factories allowed), Zone IV for "upper class residential" (Western architecture only).

Bank of China Height Restriction (1934–37)
The Bank of China's new Bund headquarters was forced to reduce from 18 to 15 floors — the British-owned Sassoon House would not permit a Chinese bank to exceed its height. The building's own institutional history describes it as "changing the landscape of a foreigner's world."

May Thirtieth Movement (1925)
Popular slogan: "Refuse foreign bank notes" (拒用外资银行钞票). Following the movement, the foreign bank market share dropped from 37% to 19% by 1936, while Chinese banks rose from 41% to 73%.

What this means for the research: The color-group structure of Shanghai Millionaire does not reflect a transposition of Waddington's British class logic onto Chinese soil. Rather, it appears to encode a spatial-nationality hierarchy that was already operative in 1930s Shanghai — a hierarchy grounded in extraterritoriality, documented in planning archives and legal codes, and visible in the everyday spatial organization of the city. The board priced British financial power (The Bund) at $350–400, French prestige (Avenue Joffre) at $180–200, and Chinese native banking (Ningpo Road, "China's Wall Street") at $140–160. Read this way, the game board becomes not merely a variant of Monopoly, but a materialized archive of treaty-port social ideology — a document in which Shanghai's extraterritorial power structure was translated into the rules of play.

Game Prices vs. Actual 1930s Land Values

SMC Land Assessment 1930 SMC Land Assessment 1933 FC Rôle foncière 1934 Mou Zhenyu Map 1933–34

How closely do the board prices track actual land values? We sourced 25,739 digitized lot-level records from Virtual Shanghai's public data repository — the Shanghai Municipal Council's Land Assessment Schedules for 1930 and 1933 (covering the International Settlement's Central, Western, Northern, and Eastern districts) and the French Concession's Rôle de la propriété foncière for 1932 and 1934. Each record lists cadastral lot number, area (mow), assessed value per mow (taels), total value, and owner nationality (under which consulate registered). We then computed a ratio for each color group: game price index ÷ actual land value index (both normalized to The Bund = 100). A ratio of 1.0 means the game price perfectly reflects the market; above 1 means the game overvalues; below 1 means it undervalues.

What the three-period comparison reveals: French Concession land values rose consistently from 1932 to 1941 (median 13,000 → 27,000 taels/mu, +108%), yet the board fixes Avenue Joffre at a static $200. The International Settlement Central district also rose sharply between 1930 and 1933 (80,000 → 110,000, +37.5%). The game's fixed-price framework — inherited from Monopoly's $60–$400 structure — freezes a pre-war snapshot. Most critically: the two financial cores (Dark Blue and Pink) are priced most accurately relative to 1933 land values, while Western residential streets (Red/Yellow) are inflated 7–8× above their market position. The board encodes not just "what the land was worth," but "what it meant to live the colonial lifestyle."

Methodology note: The original data records are digitized XLS tables from Virtual Shanghai (CC-BY 4.0), processed into a unified 25,739-row CSV by this project. Street-level actual values are estimated from district-level SMC assessment medians, not lot-by-lot matching, because the cadastral lot numbers in the SMC registers require the 1932 Cadastre Database polygon layer for precise street assignment — spatial matching remains future work. The SMC registers primarily cover foreign-consulate-registered properties; Chinese-owned lots registered under Chinese jurisdiction may be underrepresented in the assessment data, which could further bias the Pink group's "actual" estimate downward.

The Board: Price Structure

Project object data Street data JSON World of Monopoly

Shanghai Millionaire follows the broader Monopoly template: 40 squares, 8 color groups, prices increasing clockwise from GO. The localization replaces an existing property sequence with Shanghai streets while the exact Waddington/Parker Brothers route remains under investigation.

The game uses the British color scheme: Dark Blue ($350-400) as the most expensive, Purple ($60-80) as the cheapest.

Research Questions

Q1: Do game prices correlate with empirical land values? Ongoing

Collect SMC Annual Report property assessments and newspaper rental advertisements to compare with game prices. Focus on the pink group anomaly: were Tientsin/Ningpo/Honan actually lower-value, or are they undervalued in the colonial perspective?

Q2: How did the 1937-1941 "Solitary Island" period affect land values? Planned

After the 1937 Battle of Shanghai, the International Settlement became an isolated "solitary island" surrounded by Japanese occupation. Refugee influx caused dramatic shifts in land values. Does the game (likely printed 1938-1940) reflect pre-war colonial imagination or wartime reality?

Q3: What does comparison with other Monopoly versions reveal? Planned

Atlantic City (1935) and London (1936) versions have been studied for their price-value correlations. How does Shanghai's colonial context make its localization unique? Is Shanghai the only major version where localization involves colonial power asymmetry?

Q4: Who determined the value hierarchy? Planned

The game's value system reflects whose perspective? British colonial administrators? International Settlement business elites? Or is it a pragmatic adaptation of the London board's structure without local knowledge?

Historical Context

1935

Charles Darrow's Monopoly published by Parker Brothers (US). Based on Atlantic City, with prices loosely reflecting real estate values.

1936

Waddington's publishes British version with London streets. Mayfair and Park Lane correspond to actual high-value areas.

1937

Battle of Shanghai (August-November). Chinese city falls to Japanese forces. International Settlement and French Concession remain unoccupied "solitary island."

1938-1940

Massive refugee influx into the Settlement. Land values in previously "Chinese" areas appreciate dramatically. Shanghai Millionaire likely printed during this period.

1941

Pacific War begins. Japanese occupy the International Settlement (December). Game production likely ceases.

1946

Lobel brothers create handmade Shanghai Millionaire in the Hongkew designated area after liberation. Their choice of this game suggests it was known in refugee-child circles.
