Japanese Internment Camps (1943–1945)

CSUN Bell JDC Shanghai Research resources

After Pearl Harbor (December 1941), Japan occupied Shanghai's International Settlement. In January 1943, the Japanese military government began concentrating Allied civilians — British, American, Dutch, Australian, and others — into designated camps across Shanghai. Approximately 12,000 people were interned in at least 20 facilities.

Games & Culture in the Camps

Despite brutal conditions, internees organized remarkably rich cultural lives — a survival mechanism documented across multiple camps:

  • Lunghwa Academy — Herbert Huckstep and others established a school for ~350 children, using brown paper bags as writing materials
  • Jazz bands — Professional musicians from Shanghai's dance halls (Little Club, Metropole Ballroom) formed ensembles, playing standards like There'll Be Some Changes Made and Nagasaki
  • Bridge tournaments, lectures, theatrical performances — Betty Barr (last surviving Shanghai-based Lunghua internee) recalled earning her Girl Guide camping badge as "the happiest yet most ironic memory"
  • Secret radios — Two hidden radios in Lunghua camp allowed internees to follow war news

This cultural resilience provides the context for understanding why the Lobel brothers would spend effort making a board game in 1946 — even after liberation, the habit of creating entertainment from nothing persisted.

The Hongkew Ghetto

EHRI / USHMM Lobel Cheng, Power of Play Source matrix

In parallel with the Allied internment camps, approximately 20,000 Jewish refugees from Germany, Austria, and Poland lived in Shanghai — most in the Hongkew (虹口) district. After February 1943, Japan designated a "Restricted Area for Stateless Refugees" (无国籍难民指定区域), effectively creating a ghetto.

~1 sq mi Designated area
14,245 Jewish refugees confined
~100,000 Chinese residents in same area

Life in the Hongkew Ghetto was constrained but not as tightly controlled as the internment camps. Refugees could sometimes leave for work (with passes), and a rich community life developed: the Kadoorie School educated 500+ children, three German-language newspapers were published, and cultural events flourished.

The Lobel family — Siegfried and Manfred's parents — fled Berlin in September 1940 and were forced into the Hongkew designated area in 1942. The boys' 1946 hand-drawn Shanghai Millionaire was made after liberation, using surplus US Army K-ration boxes — a material artifact of their displaced lives.

Research task: compare the Lobel copy with commercial boards

If higher-resolution images or object access become available, the handmade Lobel board should be compared square by square with commercial Shanghai Millionaire copies. The key question is whether the brothers copied from a physical model, reconstructed the game from memory, or redesigned parts of it from their own Hongkew experience.

  • Street system: compare street names, order, color groups, stations, utilities, and any missing or substituted locations.
  • Value system: compare purchase prices, rents, house/hotel values, fines, salaries, and other numbers against the printed edition.
  • Rules and components: compare chance/community cards, tokens, money, title deeds, jail/free-parking equivalents, and handmade material choices.
  • Interpretive test: large divergences would make the object a rare record of how refugee children understood Shanghai, value, movement, scarcity, and social geography in the Hongkew period.

This remains a research task, not a conclusion: similarity would support copying from a model, while systematic differences would need to be documented before reading them as original design or ghetto-life evidence.

Player Communities & Circulation

Cheng Powerhouse / Ross CSUN Bell Source matrix

The new sources show that Shanghai Millionaire was not confined to a single refugee story. It circulated across overlapping wartime Shanghai communities, sometimes as a purchased commercial game, sometimes as a handmade copy, and sometimes as a remembered object of childhood and migration.

Kimberly Cheng's research on refugee children's play adds an important interpretive layer: the board helped children translate Shanghai into a playable geography of streets, prices, neighborhoods, and status. Shanghai Millionaire was therefore not only entertainment, but also a way of learning the city.

German and Austrian Jewish refugee children

Culman, the Lobel brothers, Hirsch, and the Ross family show a spectrum of use: playing, borrowing, copying, purchasing, preserving, and carrying the game abroad.

Baghdadi Jewish Shanghai families

Ellis Sassoon Jacob's memory of "many of us" playing the game links Shanghai Millionaire to older local Jewish and expatriate childhood culture, not only newly arrived refugees.

Russian and Soviet Jewish networks

Tankred Golenpolsky's recollection suggests the game also crossed into Russian-speaking Shanghai childhood memory and later Soviet-era retellings.

JDC-supported youth activities

JDC photograph evidence places the game in organized refugee children's activity settings. This is important, but should not be confused with Japanese civilian internment-camp evidence.

Research implication: the game should be framed as a wartime Shanghai object shared across expatriate, Jewish, refugee, and child communities. Private ownership may have been uneven, but play and memory spread more widely than the surviving commercial copies suggest.

Could Shanghai Millionaire Have Entered the Camps?

Lobel object Powerhouse circulation JDC photograph Evidence limits

The lists below are not direct quotations. They separate source-backed facts, contextual evidence, and our analytical inferences about whether a commercial set could have entered Japanese civilian internment camps.

Evidence against camp entry

  • Contextual evidence: Japanese civilian internment involved searches and confiscation of personal belongings; non-essential objects were vulnerable.
  • Analytical inference: food, clothing, and medicine likely took priority over games when families entered camps.
  • Analytical inference: overcrowded living space made a large board game harder to keep and use.
  • Methodological limit: Powerhouse documents postwar circulation in 1945-1946, but later circulation does not prove 1943-1945 camp access.
  • Current source audit: no document, photograph, or surviving object yet places a commercial Shanghai Millionaire inside those camps.

Evidence that keeps the question open

  • Contextual evidence: camp communities organized recreation such as bridge, theater, and music; this shows demand for play, not this specific game.
  • Contextual evidence needing source check: accounts of Lunghwa include a substantial child population, which makes youth recreation plausible.
  • Source-backed fact plus inference: EHRI/USHMM records the 1946 Lobel handmade copy from the Hongkew ghetto; it proves refugee-child copying practice, not commercial camp entry.
  • Object fact plus inference: surviving boards are physically movable, but portability alone cannot prove camp access.
Assessment: Commercial Shanghai Millionaire entering Japanese civilian internment camps remains unlikely (20–30%). This is an analytical estimate, not a cited statistic. The strongest confirmed evidence instead places the game in refugee family life, Hongkew handmade copying, and JDC-supported youth activity.
